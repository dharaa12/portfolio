01The problem

Map apps answer "what's fastest?" Nobody asks "what's greenest?"

You type Times Square into a map app, and it hands you the fastest route. The subway and bike options are a tab away, and nothing tells you that driving there from MoMA puts out about 580 g of CO₂, while the subway puts out about 103 g and a bike puts out none. So the fastest option wins, every time, by default.

The cost is invisible

Trip emissions aren't shown anywhere people plan trips, so there's nothing to compare against.

Kilograms mean nothing

Even when the number is there, "580 g CO₂" is abstract. People can't feel the difference between two numbers they have no reference for.

The payoff is delayed and shared

The greener route can cost you time now, while the benefit goes to everyone later. Without something in it for you today, habit wins.

02What it's for

For New Yorkers who already switch between subway, bike, and car.

Who

People in NYC who have real options for most trips (subway, bus, bike, car) and pick between them out of habit.

What they want

To get where they're going easily, and to feel a greener choice was worth making, without doing research first.

Why it matters

NYC's dense transit makes the low-carbon option realistic for most trips. The gap isn't access. It's that nothing makes the choice visible or rewarding.

The question I raised at the hackathon

The team wanted to build "Google Maps, but with a green route." I asked: what would make someone open our app instead? Without an answer, we'd be building a copy. That question led to the rewards marketplace.

03Competitors

Existing apps plan the trip well. None make the greener choice visible or worth it.

04My solution

Make the carbon cost visible, make it relatable, then make it pay off.

A green line alone is a copy

Google Maps could add a green route tomorrow. Rewards that you can spend locally are a reason to open GreenRoute instead. This came from the question I raised at the hackathon.

Put carbon on the route card

Driving, biking, and real NYC subway and bus routes appear side by side, each with its estimated CO₂, so the trade-off is visible at the moment of choice.

Turn kilograms into things people know

Every saving is shown in everyday terms: tree-days of CO₂ absorbed, phone charges, and miles of driving avoided, using EPA equivalency factors.

Reward the choice with something real

Greener trips earn climate points (10 per kg of CO₂ saved compared with driving) that unlock rewards from NYC businesses. Badges and a friends leaderboard keep progress visible. The rewards are examples for now: there are no live partnerships yet, and redemptions are simulated.

Start in New York

Dense transit, live MTA data, and plenty of local businesses make NYC the right place to prove the route, savings, and rewards loop before expanding. The routing already works worldwide.

A real trip: MoMA → Times SquareTimeCO₂vs. drivingClimate points
Drive · fastest4 min580 gbaseline0
Subway + walk14 min103 g82% less+4
Bike · greenest10 min0 g100% less+5

From the live app. Biking takes 6 minutes longer than driving but cuts all 580 g of CO₂, the same as avoiding about 1.4 miles of driving, or 10 tree-days of CO₂ absorption. That time cost is exactly why the greener choice needs a payoff. Points are 10 per kg of CO₂ saved. To keep them cheat-proof, the server calculates them from the trip's distance at fixed rates, so a subway trip can earn slightly less than its live CO₂ estimate suggests.

How it works

  1. Search a routeEnter where you're going, the same as in any map app.
  2. Compare every modeDrive, bike, and transit appear side by side with CO₂, tagged fastest or greenest.
  3. Take the greener tripEach green trip is logged with the CO₂ it saved and adds climate points to your account.
  4. Spend your pointsBrowse rewards from NYC businesses, like a free subway ride or a free matcha, on a map.

Keeping people coming back

  1. LeaderboardRanks people by total CO₂ saved, globally or among friends.
  2. BadgesMilestones from a first green trip to 500 climate points give people a next goal.

06What I learned

The most useful question came before any code.

Ask "why would someone switch?" first

Our first idea worked, but nobody had a reason to use it over Google Maps. Answering that question shaped the whole product, and I now ask it before any feature work.

A demo hides what a product needs

Sample data was enough to pitch. Accounts, live transit data, and slow phone connections only showed up when I built it for real, and each one changed the product.

Start small enough to prove it

Launching in New York first, with dense transit and plenty of local businesses, made the idea testable. Trying to work everywhere at once would not have.

New to BeReal?

A photo app built around one honest moment a day.

BeReal is a social app that took off in 2022. Its idea is simple: share what you're really doing, not a polished highlight. Fewer people use it daily now, and the challenge was to bring them back.

  1. Once a day, at a random time, everyone gets the same alert: "Time to BeReal."
  2. You have 2 minutes to take a photo with your front and back cameras at once.
  3. You have to post before you can see your friends' posts.
  4. Post after the 2 minutes and your photo is labeled late, like "3 hours late."
What I did
  • I analyzed what users say about BeReal on TikTok, Reddit, and App Store reviews, and found that the timing of the daily alert was the biggest reason people miss posts and drift away.
  • I came up with 7 feature ideas, scored them by reach, impact, confidence, and effort, and picked Unavailable Hours.
  • I wrote the product requirements, built a clickable Figma prototype, and tested it with college-age users.
The constraint that shaped every decision

Grow users while competing with Instagram, Snapchat, and TikTok without copying their addictive design.

01The problem

The alert is random. People's schedules aren't.

"Time to BeReal" goes off in the middle of a lecture. By the time class ends, the 2 minutes are long gone, and the post goes up with "14 hr late" above it. One user said it feels like failing a test they never signed up for.

Randomness is the product

Users call the random alert "both the best and worst part of the app." Removing it would remove what makes BeReal special.

Most growth tactics are off-limits

Streaks, urgency, and endless feeds would grow usage by going against BeReal's core values of being real and spontaneous.

The penalty is public

A missed window doesn't just cost a post. The late label stays on the photo for friends to see.

02What it's for

For students and recent grads whose days run on a schedule.

Who

College students and people 1–2 years out of school. Their days are blocked into classes, shifts, and meetings.

What they want

To post on time without stepping out of class or a meeting, and without the stress of a late label.

Why it matters

When the alert keeps clashing with their schedule, posting becomes a recurring bad experience. That's when people stop opening the app.

Key takeaway

Timing is the main reason people miss posts and drift away. Fixing it removes a reason to leave, without adding pressure to stay.

03Competitors

Rivals keep users with pressure and algorithms. BeReal needs a different lever.

DirectInstagram, Snapchat IndirectTikTok, Locket SubstituteiMessage, WhatsApp, Discord

I chose Instagram as my main benchmark. It meets the same need (staying close to friends) at a similar scale, and its engagement-driven design is the sharpest contrast to BeReal.

The lesson worth borrowing: Instagram's Following and Favorites feeds are popular because people want some control. BeReal could offer control over timing, without giving up the randomness.

05My solution

Let people block when they can't post, never choose when they will.

Unavailable Hours lets users block off chunks of time when they don't want the daily alert. It still fires once a day at a random moment, just outside the blocked hours.

The key decision

I weighed two ways to give people control and reviewed them in my check-ins with the program's project manager. The deciding question was which option keeps the alert unpredictable.

Option 1 · Not chosen

Pick a preferred window

"Notify me sometime between 6 and 9 PM."

  • Turns BeReal into a scheduling tool
  • People could plan their post, which breaks "spontaneous"
  • Moves BeReal toward the curated experience Instagram already owns
Option 2 · Chosen

Block unavailable time

"Don't notify me from 9 AM to 5 PM."

  • Works like Do Not Disturb or a busy block on a calendar, so people already get it
  • The alert stays random within all other hours
  • Users control when it can't happen, never when it will

Blocks, not exact times

Time is set in chunks, not to the minute. Each block is at least 3 hours, and total blocked time stays under about 21 hours, so the alert always has room to land.

Small to build

No change to the random alert itself. It adds a settings and validation layer on top, which is why it tied for the lowest effort score of all 7 ideas.

Private and optional

Friends never see your blocked hours, and you can turn the feature off anytime.

One user request I said no to

A tester asked for a heads-up when a block ends. I didn't add it because it would tell people the alert's window just opened, which makes BeReal predictable and breaks the "spontaneous, not addictive" pillar.

The prototype flow

  1. Learn itAdded after testingA short intro explains the feature.
  2. Find itLives in Settings, marked New.
  3. Turn it onOne main toggle.
  4. Set blocksAdd one or more busy windows.
  5. ConfirmAdded after testingShows that your settings saved.
06Business case

Every skipped post is a day with no ad views.

BeReal now makes money from ads shown in the feed. Since you have to post before you can see your friends' feed, a missed alert costs more than one photo. It means no feed visit and no ad views that day.

  1. Alert lands at a bad timeIn class, at work, or in a meeting
  2. User skips the postOr posts late and gets the "late" label
  3. Feed stays lockedNo post means no friends' posts to view
  4. No ad viewsAds only appear in the feed
~50%drop in daily users from the October 2022 peak to early 2023, per Apptopia

Why now

Voodoo bought BeReal for €500 million in June 2024, and BeReal launched ads in the US in April 2025. Keeping people opening the app each day now affects revenue directly.

Low cost

It doesn't change the random alert itself, just adds a settings layer on top. It tied for the lowest effort score of the 7 ideas I ranked.

Protects what advertisers pay for

Advertisers come for an authentic, mostly Gen Z audience. This feature grows usage by removing stress, not by adding pressure that would make BeReal feel like every other app.

What it could mean

An illustration, not a forecast: about 20M of BeReal's reported users aren't near-daily. If the feature helped just 1% of them post two more days a week, that would add roughly 20 million more days of feed visits a year.

08What I learned

Removing friction can grow a product without adding pressure.

Familiar beats clever

Most testers understood Unavailable Hours right away because it works like Do Not Disturb. One didn't, so I added a short intro. Even a familiar idea needs a little context.

Feedback is input, not instructions

I said no to a feature a tester asked for because it broke BeReal's "spontaneous, not addictive" pillar. Listening to users and protecting a core principle can point in different directions.

Wanting a feature isn't using it

"83% would recommend it" shows people want it, not that they'd post more. That's why my next step is a live test that measures real behavior.

09What I'd do next

Fix the gaps, then measure real usage.

  • Make adding and removing time blocks work the same on every screen.
  • Run a second, larger round of testing on the refined prototype.
  • Launch to a test group first and compare daily posting and 7- and 30-day return rates against users without the feature.
  • Confirm the 3-hour minimum and 21-hour cap with real usage data.
  • Watch whether some users feel it makes the app less random over time.
  • If it works, apply the same "limit without scheduling" idea to late-post reminders.
